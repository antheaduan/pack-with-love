Why we do it this way

Fundraising is not asking for money

It is a complete real-world project.

Across generations of civic education, fundraising has been understood as training in leadership, communication, entrepreneurship, and citizenship. On the surface, a child is raising money. Underneath, they come face to face with one very real question:

“I believe this is worth doing — but why would anyone else support me?”

Answering that question honestly is the whole curriculum. Done well, fundraising is a complete chain of real work — from discovering a need all the way to reporting back to every supporter.

The world our children are entering is changing quickly

A poster, a website, a video, a budget spreadsheet, a fundraising letter — work that once took a team and real technical skill, AI now helps produce in hours or minutes. So the gap between people is no longer mainly “who can make it?” It is becoming three questions AI cannot answer for anyone.

Who can make people care?

AI can

write the fundraising email.

It cannot

make the reader care.

Who can earn trust?

AI can

find a hundred potential donors.

It cannot

make one of them believe “this young person is serious, and I want to support them.”

Who can organize people around an idea?

AI can

generate a beautiful presentation.

It cannot

recruit a friend, coordinate a team, or stand in front of a room and take responsibility for the answer: why does this matter?

Education was Knowledge→ Skill→ Produce something
It is becoming Problem→ Judgment→ Communication→ Mobilization→ Execution

Fundraising is one of the few school-age experiences that strings all five together — which is why we treat it as a durably human skill set: the abilities that appreciate, not depreciate, as AI advances.

The whole chain, in four moves

The Four Moves

Every participant — one child with a lemonade stand or a whole class with a data project — walks the same four moves, and every dollar counts twice at once: on their own progress bar and on the shared goal.

Move 1

Claim — Decide what's yours.

“What can I actually do — and how much of this is mine to carry?”

A claim starts with an honest inventory, not a wish. Brainstorm what you already have:

  • A talent you can perform — violin or guitar at the farmers' market, a dance or choir piece at a community event, a mini-recital for neighbors.
  • Something you can make — a bake sale, succulent planters, a lemonade stand, handmade cards or bracelets, holiday cookies.
  • Something you can resell — bubble tea ordered from a shop and sold at a school event, cold drinks at a game, snacks at a fair. You buy at one price and sell at another, so the math is real: what it cost, what you charge, what is actually left for the project.
  • Work you can offer — a car wash, yard work and leaf raking, dog walking, gift-wrapping in December, tutoring younger students.
  • People you already know — your parents' company, your church, your neighbors, your teachers and coaches.
  • A team you can gather — two friends splitting the roles, a class challenge, a school club you found yourself.

Then size the claim to match what you found — always in the project's real units, not just dollars: ten backpacks alone, twenty-five with two friends, a hundred through a club. A real claim answers four questions: What can I do? Who do I know? How many hours do I have? Alone, team, or club?

A parent registers the claim from their own ACO account, and ACO reads it once before the page goes live.

Move 2

Plan — Turn the claim into a working plan.

“I know what I'm doing and how much I'm raising. Now — exactly how?”

The goal is set, the approach is chosen. Now every detail gets decided. A bake sale: bake it yourself or buy wholesale? What does each item cost, and what do you charge? Where — the school gate, church, the farmers' market? Which Saturday? Who has to give permission? A violin performance: what repertoire, what sign explains the cause, who watches the cash box? A team: who promotes, who contacts, who records, who executes?

Every plan ends with the same three artifacts: the ask (what you will say), the schedule (when and where), and the tracking sheet (how you will know where you stand).

Move 3

Do — Ask, hear no, keep going.

“Can I keep going when it's harder than the plan said?”

The plan is on paper. Now it meets the real world. The asks happen for real — face to face, at the school gate, at the market. Some people stop and listen. Some say no. The tracker gets updated either way, and the plan gets revised, not abandoned. The story you drafted in Move 2 is now told out loud, to a stranger, in your own voice.

How cash becomes progress

A bake sale collects cash; the page tracks online. One path keeps the two matched.

CountAfter each activity, count the cash with a parent and enter the gross amount and the costs in your tracking sheet the same day.
Net itWhat moves your bar is what is actually left for the project — sales minus what the supplies cost. Direct donations have no costs and count in full. (One exception: the Data to Impact class's materials are bought with tuition, which never touches the bar — so the class's sales count at full value.)
Give itOnce a week a parent gives that week's net through your page's own donation link — it sits in their ACO account with a Copy button beside it. Money that arrives that way lands on your bar and the shared goal together.
Match upEach week your tracking sheet and your page should agree. If they don't, find out why that week, not at the end. That is what “every dollar traced” means in practice.

Resize — the midpoint checkpoint

About halfway through, review your claim with a parent or coach and answer one question honestly: am I ahead, on track, or behind — and why?

AheadClaim more. The extra comes from the part nobody has claimed yet.
BehindResize down. What you release returns to the shared pool, where the campaign and the others carry it. Resizing down is not failing — it is Move 1 done again with better information, because the inventory you take now includes real data.
On trackKeep going. Update your tracker and say so out loud to your team.

Changing a claim sends the page back to ACO for one quick read before it is public again — so do it at the checkpoint, not piecemeal.

Move 4

Report — Close the loop.

“Does every supporter know what their trust built?”

Every dollar traced. Every giver thanked. The results presented — to supporters, to the team, to the next group of participants. The same story is told one final time: not “please support us,” but “here is what your trust built.”

Move 2 ended with three artifacts. So does Move 4.

AccountOne page: total raised, costs spent, net delivered, and how it compares to the claim. Signed by you, checked by a parent or coach.
ThanksEvery supporter named in your tracker hears from you personally — a note, a card, or a thank you in person. No supporter learns the outcome from silence.
ImpactOne sentence in your own words: “Your trust became ___.” It is what you present when the project closes, and what the next group reads when they start Move 1.

One story, told three times

Notice where Storytelling appears: drafted in the plan, told out loud in the doing, completed in the report. That is not repetition — it is how the skill gets built. The full chain runs inside these four moves, and every practice tagged along the way has its place in it.

How claims grow

Year 1Claim 10 backpacks, alone.

The projects change. The claims grow. That is the formation model.

The project clock

One year, one calendar

Every family sees the same calendar. All dates are approximate — the real project comes first.

  • ≈ Late Sept – mid Oct 2026

  • ≈ Mid Oct 2026 – Feb 28, 2027

    Move 3 — Do

    The full season — including Sales Events I & II in January — with weekly reconciliation.

  • ≈ Mid Dec 2026

    Resize checkpoint

    Claims resized or confirmed before the holiday-season push.

  • ≈ Nov – Dec 2026

    Production order placed

    Funded by the bridge advance, per the supplier's confirmed lead time; ocean shipping takes 5–6 weeks.

  • ≈ March 1, 2027

    Funds close

    All money in; the $5,300 bar is final.

  • ≈ March 1 – 6, 2027

    Move 4 — Report

    Final accounts, thank-yous, impact statements.

  • March 7, 2027

    Final Impact Showcase

    “Your trust became ___ backpacks.” — the final number, presented.

Two clocks, one bridge

This project runs on two clocks. The production clock is fixed by the mission: backpacks must be ordered, shipped, and cleared before March 2027 — so production never waits on the progress bar. It is guaranteed by a bridge advance from the founding family, paid to the supplier on schedule.

The fundraising clock runs the full season, so the January sales events, the holiday season, and every late gift all count. Donations repay the bridge as they arrive; whatever remains uncovered at close becomes the founding family's own gift to the project.

No child owes the gap. A claim is a commitment to effort, not a debt.
