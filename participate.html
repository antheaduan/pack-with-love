Why we do fundraising

Fundraising is not asking for money.
It is a complete real-world project.

Our charter gives Pack with Love a double purpose — and the second half is written for the students: “to help young people grow through meaningful service — carrying real, age-appropriate responsibility in creativity, teamwork, and care. We don't measure success only by how many items we give.”

Across generations of civic education, fundraising has been understood as training in leadership, communication, entrepreneurship, and citizenship. On the surface, a child is raising money. Underneath, they come face to face with one very real question:

“I believe this is worth doing — but why would anyone else support me?”

Answering that question honestly is the whole curriculum. Done well, fundraising is a complete chain of real work — from discovering a need all the way to reporting back to every supporter.

The framework

To turn that purpose into practice,
we designed the Four Moves

Most families think fundraising means asking people for money. We teach a four-move method instead — the same four moves for a lemonade stand or a year-long data project. What changes is depth.

How it works: every project has one shared goal; you claim a portion on your own page, and every dollar you raise counts on both bars at once — they never disagree, because they are the same money. A claim is a commitment to effort, not a debt. Each card below opens into the full guidance.

Move 1 · Claim

Say what you'll carry

Start with an honest inventory, not a wish — what can you do, who do you know — then size the claim in real units (ten backpacks alone, twenty-five with two friends), and a parent confirms it. Ambition, not a guess.

You produce: a live claim page with its own progress bar and donation link.

Open the full guidance — the inventory & the Matching Gifts rule

“What can I actually do — and how much of this is mine to carry?” Brainstorm what you already have:

  • A talent you can perform — violin or guitar at the farmers' market, a dance or choir piece at a community event, a mini-recital for neighbors.
  • Something you can make — a bake sale, succulent planters, a lemonade stand, handmade cards or bracelets, holiday cookies.
  • Something you can resell — bubble tea sold at a school event, cold drinks at a game. You buy at one price and sell at another, so the math is real: what it cost, what you charge, what is actually left for the project.
  • Work you can offer — a car wash, yard work, dog walking, gift-wrapping in December, tutoring younger students.
  • People you already know — your parents' company (ask about donation matching), your church, your neighbors, your teachers and coaches.
  • A team you can gather — two friends splitting the roles, a class challenge, a school club you found yourself.

A real claim answers four questions: What can I do? Who do I know? How many hours do I have? Alone, team, or club? Every claim is confirmed by a parent before it goes live.

The Matching Gifts rule Employer matching counts in full toward the shared goal — and on your personal page it appears as its own labeled line (“employer match”), alongside — not inside — what you raised yourself. Both numbers are yours to be proud of; keeping them side by side keeps clear exactly how much you carried with your own hands.

Move 2 · Plan

Three small artifacts

Every plan is just three things a child can own: the Ask — one honest paragraph answering “why would anyone support me?”; the Schedule; and the Tracking Sheet.

You produce: the Ask · the Schedule · the Tracking Sheet.

Open the full guidance — what “every detail decided” looks like

“I know what I'm doing and how much I'm raising. Now — exactly how?”

A bake sale: bake it yourself or buy wholesale? What does each item cost, and what do you charge? Where — the school gate, church, the farmers' market? Which Saturday? Who has to give permission? A violin performance: what repertoire, what sign explains the cause, who watches the cash box? A team: who promotes, who contacts, who records, who executes?

However different the activities, every plan ends with the same three artifacts — small enough for a child to own, real enough to run a season on.

Move 3 · Do

The season of real work

The asks happen for real — face to face. Some people say no; the tracker gets updated either way, and the plan gets revised, not abandoned. Weekly reconciliation counts net, not gross, and the mid-season RESIZE checkpoint keeps every claim honest.

You produce: a truthful tracking sheet, week after week.

Open the full guidance — the reconciliation path & RESIZE

“Can I keep going when it's harder than the plan said?”

How cash becomes progress — the reconciliation path:

  1. Count together. After each activity, count the cash with a parent and enter the gross amount and the costs in your tracking sheet the same day.
  2. Net, not gross. What moves your progress bar is what is actually left for the project — sales minus what the supplies cost. (Direct donations have no costs; they count in full.)
  3. Deposit weekly. A parent converts the week's net cash through your page's donation link (or delivers it to ACO with your name on the envelope). It lands on your progress and the shared $5,300 goal at the same time — the bars never disagree.
  4. Reconcile. Once a week, your tracking sheet and your page should match. If they don't, find out why this week, not at the end. That is “every dollar traced,” in practice.

At every event, a supervising adult holds the money — students plan, present, sell, and record.

RESIZE — the midpoint checkpoint (≈ mid-December) “Halfway through, is my claim still the right size?” Review with a parent or coach: Ahead? Claim more from the unclaimed pool. Behind? Resize down — the released portion returns to the shared pool. Resizing is Move 1 done again with better information, not failing. On track? Keep going, and say so out loud to your team.

The safety rule, plainly: no child owes their claim. Every unmet portion is covered by the shared campaign. What you owe is only this: keep going, track honestly, revise rather than abandon.

Move 4 · Report

Answer for the result

Every dollar traced, every giver thanked, the result presented — not “please support us,” but “here is what your trust built.” No one who gave learns the outcome from silence.

You produce: final account · thank-yous · your impact statement.

Open the full guidance — the closing three artifacts

“Does every supporter know what their trust built?” Move 2 ends with three artifacts. So does Move 4:

  1. The final account — one page: total raised, costs spent, net delivered, and how it compares to the claim. Signed by you, checked by a parent or coach.
  2. The thank-yous — every supporter, named in your tracker, hears from you personally: a note, a card, or a face-to-face thank you.
  3. The impact statement — one sentence in your own words: “Your trust became ___ backpacks.” You present it at the Final Impact Showcase — and the next group of participants will read it when they start Move 1.

Two clocks, one bridge

Production never waits on the progress bar

The production order is guaranteed by a bridge advance from the founding family, and donations repay the bridge as they arrive. If the season ends short, the gap becomes the founding family's own gift — never a child's burden.

No child owes the gap. A claim is a commitment to effort, not a debt.

The world is changing quickly

Three questions AI cannot answer for anyone

A poster, a website, a budget spreadsheet, a fundraising letter — work that once took a team, AI now helps produce in minutes. So the gap between people is no longer “who can make it?” It is becoming:

Who can make people care?

AI can

write the fundraising email.

It cannot

make the reader care.

Who can earn trust?

AI can

find a hundred potential donors.

It cannot

make one of them believe “this young person is serious, and I want to support them.”

Who can organize people?

AI can

generate a beautiful presentation.

It cannot

recruit a friend, coordinate a team, or stand in front of a room and take responsibility for “why does this matter?”

Knowledge → Skill → Produce something ⟶ Problem → Judgment → Communication → Mobilization → Execution

Fundraising strings all five together — a durably human skill set: abilities that appreciate, not depreciate, as AI advances. The Four Moves above, and the twelve capabilities they train, are exactly that skill set — which is why we treat a child's claim as a curriculum, not a chore.
